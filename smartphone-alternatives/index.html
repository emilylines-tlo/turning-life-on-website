Smartphone alternatives

Start with the simplest device that works.

What each option costs, what it does, and what goes wrong with it.

16 devices compared Prices verified 13 Oct 2026 Devices you can borrow

Parents need a way to reach their kids, and we support that. Our concern with smartphones isn't calling and texting. It's that they give kids constant, unrestricted access to social media, gaming and the internet, and crowd out face-to-face time and other healthy activities.

There's no research-backed “safe age” to give your child a smartphone. Match the device to your child's maturity level, not their age.

Start here

What are you trying to solve?

Most of us start by asking which phone to buy. The better first question is what the device is for.

The simplest device that solves it

A home phone, not a phone for them.

If the need is “call me from the house,” a landline covers it. About $75–140 for the phone, then up to $10 a month. It is also an easy way for kids to call grandparents, cousins and family, which tends to happen more when the phone is sitting there in the kitchen.

The simplest device that solves it

A basic phone, or a simple watch.

Both cover this one. A watch puts location and calling on their wrist, and it also sits there all day buzzing at them, which is some of what you were trying to avoid. A basic phone goes in a bag and can be ignored for an hour. On kid watches, mind the catch: several ship a game layer you cannot switch off, and two limit texting to canned replies, which suits an 8-year-old and frustrates a 12-year-old.

For the phone route, Dumb Wireless is the easiest place to start. They stock AGM, Light Phone, Punkt and Sunbeam, and they sell service on the T-Mobile network to go with them, which saves a lot of the carrier runaround.

Dumb Wireless
Worth sitting with

This one is your call.

Ask a 12-year-old whether being the only one without a phone will hurt their friendships and the answer is yes, every time. They believe it, and at that age it feels true. What we get to weigh and they cannot: most of what the phone buys is entry to a group chat, and the hours it takes have to come from somewhere. It can settle one problem and quietly create others, trading the afternoons that went to a practice, a band, or a friend's kitchen table for time on a screen.

“Today, the average 18 year old in the United States is on pace to spend 93% of their remaining free time looking at a screen.”

Dino Ambrosi, TEDxLagunaBlancaSchool, February 2023

What lowers the pressure is other families making the same call. The Wait Until 8th pledge lets parents in the same grade at the same school agree to wait together, and it only takes effect once ten families from that grade and school have signed. Being the only one is a different situation from being one of many, and that gap is the thing you can close.

Delay the smartphone
The simplest device that solves it

A basic phone, once you have read the group chat section.

Calls and texts cover the logistics fine. The part that catches families out is group chats, both what gets said in them and how badly they work on a basic phone. Worth reading before you buy.

The ladder

Start at rung one. Only go further if you have to.

Every rung solves a real need, and none of them is a default. Work out which problem you are solving, then stop at the first rung that solves it. Plenty of families run two, a watch on the wrist and a basic phone in the bag. Most of these cost less than the phone you are trying to avoid.

The rungs are ordered by how much of the internet reaches your child, not by price.

Past the last rung

An iPhone with parental controls Not recommended

The one families drift into, because there is already an old iPhone in a drawer. Screen Time limits get bypassed by changing the date, deleting and reinstalling apps, or using a browser inside another app, and kids trade the workarounds with each other.

An unlocked smartphone Worst option

Everything at once, in a 10-year-old's pocket. If this is where you land, let it be a decision rather than a default.

One test for any device

Does it have a browser, an app store, or a feed? That is the difference between a way to reach your kid and something built to hold their attention, whatever shape it comes in.

Before you buy anything that texts

Group chats come with nearly every device here

A limited phone or watch takes social media, the open web and an app store off the table. Group chats stay. Pile-ons that would never happen face to face, kids added and removed to make a point, a photo shared with 30 people in a second. Most of it happens with no adult aware, and the kid on the receiving end usually says nothing at home.

  1. Can I see what is being said? Some devices show you every message and some show you nothing. It varies more than almost anything else here, and it rarely appears on the box.
  2. Does my kid know I can see it? Say so up front. Reading their messages in secret costs more than it gains.

One more thing about basic phones. When someone in a group is not on iMessage, the thread drops to older technology for everyone: messages land late, out of order, or not at all. Worth talking through before you buy, and plenty of families decide the simpler device is worth the friction.

Next steps

Whichever device you choose, if any, set up a family media plan and basic phone etiquette rules, like no devices in bedrooms, at the dinner table or in the car. Another easy rule: People Come First.

Also, don't be afraid of free-range parenting. Many families let their children walk home from school or around their community with no device at all, just like kids did in the '70s, '80s and '90s. It's a personal decision that depends on where you live, your child's age and how prepared they are.
